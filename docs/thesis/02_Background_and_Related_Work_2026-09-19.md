## 2.1 Offline learning in a stochastic finite MDP

Let `M = (S, A, P, r, gamma, rho_0)` be a discounted finite Markov decision
process. The learner observes trajectories generated by a behavior policy
`beta` and cannot query the transition kernel, collect new actions, or alter the
state distribution. Two buffers with the same nominal size may therefore carry
very different information. One may repeatedly cover the state-action pairs
that determine return; another may spend most samples in irrelevant or redundant
regions. In a stochastic MDP, finite buffers also differ in successor diversity,
reward dispersion, and trajectory outcomes even under the same behavior policy.

Offline RL methods address this problem from the policy-learning side.
Pessimistic value methods lower estimates for poorly supported actions; safe
policy-improvement methods constrain deviation from the behavior policy; and
batch-constrained or conservative neural methods suppress unsupported action
selection. These approaches answer how to fit a policy from a given buffer. The
present work asks an earlier question: what can the buffer predict about which
learner will succeed at a future data budget?

## 2.2 Coverage, uncertainty, and learner dependence

Coverage is necessary but not sufficient. Counts determine whether conditional
means can be estimated, while transition and reward variance determine how fast
those estimates concentrate. Trajectory quality matters because return-bearing
states may be observed only in a small part of the log. The relevant coverage is
also learner dependent: a conservative learner may tolerate missing actions by
staying near the behavior policy, whereas an aggressive value learner may be
more sensitive to extrapolation or estimation error.

Offline sample-complexity theory expresses this dependence through coverage,
concentrability, horizon, and approximation assumptions. Such results identify
structural quantities that a valid analysis cannot ignore. They do not directly
provide a calibrated, per-buffer prediction of the empirical budget at which a
specific implementation reaches a target return. This thesis treats that gap as
an empirical prediction problem rather than substituting a worst-case bound for
an observed learning curve.

## 2.3 Effective horizon

Effective horizon is an instance-dependent account of how much lookahead and
leaf evidence are needed before a greedy decision becomes reliable. Its key
insight is that nominal episode length can overstate decision difficulty: if
short lookahead plus accurate leaf values determine the action, the effective
horizon is short even when trajectories are long.

An offline extension must change the source of evidence. Interactive effective
horizon can request new rollouts. Offline evidence is fixed by the behavior
distribution. An offline formulation must therefore represent both planning
depth and the rate at which logged samples distinguish the actions that matter.
Zero behavior probability is a boundary of identification, not merely a large
finite penalty.

## 2.4 Action identification and empirical-Bernstein rates

Best-action identification relates the difficulty of a comparison to its gap,
outcome variance, and sampling allocation. For two supported actions with gap
`Delta`, conditional variances `v_a`, and behavior frequencies `d_a`, a
Bernstein-style future sample scale contains a quadratic term proportional to
`sum_a v_a/d_a / Delta^2` and a bounded-tail term proportional to
`sum_a 1/d_a / Delta`. The structure has the expected monotonicity: smaller gaps,
higher stochastic variance, and rarer actions increase the requirement.

OEH-v3 uses this structure as a local plug-in forecast. It does not inherit the
stopping guarantees of a sequential bandit algorithm because the action values
are produced by a data-dependent MDP model and Bellman backups. Its contribution
is the connection between comparison error and return impact across states.

## 2.5 Dataset characterization and learning-curve prediction

Dataset-centric offline RL studies commonly summarize trajectory quality,
state-action coverage, behavior-policy composition, and transition diversity.
Learning-curve extrapolation and algorithm selection add a supervised layer:
features from a partial run or pilot are used to predict final performance or
choose a method. That layer creates a leakage risk. If preprocessing, feature
choice, hyperparameter tuning, or comparator selection sees the test MDP's
outcome, an apparently strong selector may only encode the evaluation set.

The appropriate unit of independence in this benchmark is the base MDP. Five
behavior policies generated from one MDP share topology, rewards, and transition
dynamics; they must remain in the same fold and bootstrap cluster. Pilot streams
must also be independent of the training streams used to create empirical
requirements.

## 2.6 Position of this thesis

The work connects three lines of research: instance-dependent planning depth,
offline identification under partial coverage, and learner-conditional outcome
prediction. Its principal object is neither a new policy optimizer nor a new
worst-case bound. It is an auditable test of whether a pilot-only risk profile
adds predictive and decision value after simpler dataset characteristics are
already available.

The closest baselines play different roles. CFQI and IQL provide the principal
learning curves; behavior cloning is a low-complexity sanity baseline.
Pi_b-SPIBB tests safe policy improvement in the same tabular logs. Discounted
VI-LCB represents pessimistic model-based planning. Discrete BCQ and CQL check
whether the method ordering is specific to tabular learners, within a deliberately
limited neural compute budget. None of these algorithms validates OEH by itself;
OEH is validated only through its pre-specified incremental prediction and
selection endpoints.
